Five pieces turned inward: the compass applied to myself.

In this chapter I look at myself. My five stories show where my way of leading comes from: Warns, my family, my old coach Daniel, my time as a ski instructor in Austria and a difficult year in Groningen (1.1). #### Story 3. Mentorship, Daniel

| Compass position | West, wisdom |
| --- | --- |
| Setting | My football club, under 17 and under 19 |

Daniel Ybema was my coach and trainer in the under 17 and under 19. He stood in front of the group with confidence, explained things clearly and always knew what he wanted. One day the head coach wanted to take some of our players to the first team. Daniel asked if they would start in the first eleven. The answer was no. This story belongs on West because Daniel passed on wisdom about leading: a leader fights for his people. Over time I also tested that wisdom against my own view, and today I would add one step, asking first.

#### Story 4. Call to Adventure, ski instructor in Austria

| Compass position | West, wisdom |

| Setting | Austria, three years ago |

Three years ago I went to Austria to work as a ski instructor. It was scary. I came to a country with a language I did not speak well, people I did not know, and very little information beforehand. #### 1.1.2 My view of leadership, three times over

**View 1. Start of the minor.** In my notes from the first lesson, I wrote that I wanted to find out what kind of leader I am, and how I can help people get the most out of themselves. The leader I would follow was someone who is confident, knows what they are doing and has experience in the subject. That looks a lot like my old coach Daniel (see Story 3). I saw myself as a leader who leads by example and keeps oversight. What I needed to develop was standing in front of a group of people I do not know well, with confidence.

**View 2. During the minor.** To be honest, I did not stop to look at my view of leadership halfway through the minor. But during the film night, something did shift. We watched The Big Short, and I saw Michael Burry as a leader of the previous generation. He was confident and he was right, but I did not see him explain to the people around him why he did what he did. That made me see that confidence and knowing what you are doing, my picture of a good leader at the start, are not enough. A leader also has to be able to explain his choices.

**View 3. Now, at the end of this block.** Now, a good leader for me first looks through someone else's kaleidoscope. I ask what someone finds important or thinks they are good at, I look for myself, and then I decide and explain why. I saw how this works with a new boy in my football team. I asked him where he liked to play, saw in the warm-up that midfield fitted him better, explained it to him, and he gave two assists. I kept something from Daniel and from my first view: a leader still has to be confident and make decisions. But I now add two steps, asking first and explaining afterwards. I also learned that this counts for myself. When others know more than I do, I have to ask them, instead of trying to do everything myself. As for standing in front of people I do not know well, at the film night I was with three people I did not know, and we clicked from the first moment. The soapbox at the exhibition will be the real test.

But I had little experience with it myself. Looking back, that part of my question was more a gap than something I already knew.

### 1.4 Reflection on the serious games

I reflect on two serious games. Game 1 is the International Trade Game; I chose it because I was not there for The Big Picture. Game 2 is Change Journey.

**Game 1 · The International Trade Game: what I did.** We played the International Trade Game in week 1 of the minor. The group was split into six teams, the "countries". Every country got an envelope that could hold paper, pencils, scissors and rulers. You earned money by cutting shapes from the paper and selling them to the bank, at prices that went up and down. When we found out that not everyone had the same things in their envelope, our group had a lot of paper but no scissors. I went to all the other countries first. They all asked very high prices for their scissors. I went back to my group and discussed it with them. My plan was to use a pair of scissors for a set time instead of buying one, so to rent it. That became possible when we got the doubler, a token you could put on one sheet of paper to double its value. I also noticed that I wanted our country to work in a structured way. One person started making triangles from our paper, and I went with someone else to negotiate for scissors to rent.

**What I would not have predicted.** I am always very enthusiastic in games like this and want to get to work straight away. I did not know the people in my group yet. Still, I first went round all the countries, discussed a plan with my group and divided the tasks, instead of just starting.

**Game 2 · Change Journey: what I did.** In Change Journey, our team was a consultancy firm. A nursery school was in trouble, and we had to talk to all the stakeholders and then present a plan to Sod, a toddler, in three minutes and in a very simple way. Someone in our group again started thinking of solutions straight away and began working on a poster. I tried to first interview all the stakeholders, and only then come up with a solution. After every interview, that one person went straight back to solutions. After I said it twice, the group went along with me. I also tried to make the group wait with searching for pictures for the poster.

**What this says about my leadership.** In both games I wanted to understand the situation first. I went round all the countries before we traded, and I wanted to interview all the stakeholders before we chose a solution. I find that unstructured teamwork works badly. I like it when everyone is on the same line about how we work, and stays there. That is also what I propose for the restocking team at Plus Stavoren (see 3.2). On the compass, I stood on East: first finding out what the situation asks of us.

### 1.5 Your 80,000 hours

About 80,000 working hours lie ahead of me. Three paths are realistically open: a master in sustainable entrepreneurship, a job in HR, or my own company that implements AI systems for other businesses. In this piece I look mostly at the third path, my own AI company. It is one direction I might go in, but nothing is fixed yet. Below I read that working life along the compass, in about 800 words.

**East, the situation.** I am a second-year HRM student and recently finished my HR internship at Home Center Wolvega. After my studies, I can do a master in sustainable entrepreneurship, take a job in HR, or start my own company that implements AI systems for other businesses. The third pulls at me most. At my internship I built an HR chatbot that answers simple questions from employees. It took no one's job. It lowered the pressure on HR, and my colleague used the time for more important things, including onboarding. At home I built a trading bot. I can build these systems. The master serves mainly my own interest. In HR, employees gain when I help them grow. In an AI company, my clients and I gain, but their employees could lose work. I value helping people grow, working with AI and seeing a business grow.

**My central question.** *Is it right for me to build a business on automating other people's work, if what drives me is helping people grow?*

**West, wisdom.** Edgar Schein (1928–2023) was a professor of organisational psychology at MIT. He followed a group of MIT management graduates through the first part of their careers. When they had to make hard choices, they kept falling back on the same thing. Schein called this a career anchor, a set of talents, motives and values that you see as your own and would not give up if you had to choose (Schein, 1996). He named eight anchors, and most people have one or two. Schein speaks from organisational psychology and career theory, not from ethics. His frame shows me that my three paths are not just three jobs. They pull at two anchors. The AI company fits entrepreneurial creativity, building something of my own. HR fits service and dedication to a cause, helping people grow. So my question is not AI against HR. It is about which anchor decides when they collide. What Schein overlooks is the other side of the work. His anchors ask what fits me, not what my work does to the people on the receiving end, like the employees whose tasks I automate.

**South, self-knowledge.** Schein fits because my question is a career choice, but he stops where the moral question starts. My values collide, my own business and security against other people's growth and jobs. At first I said helping people grow weighs more. But would I turn down a client while my company needs the money? Honestly, no. Then my own needs come first. The philosopher Bernard Williams (1929–2003) sharpens this with the example of George, a chemist who cannot find work and is offered a job in a laboratory that does research into chemical and biological weapons. George is against those weapons, but if he refuses, someone else will take the job and do it with more zeal (Williams, 1973). My reasoning would be that if I refuse, another AI company builds it anyway, maybe without thinking about the employees. Williams shows that this argument can justify almost anything. For me it only holds if I really do it differently. I have to look through the employees' kaleidoscope and help the client use the freed-up time for people, like my chatbot did with onboarding. If I do not, it is a temptation, not a value. I picture Marja, 52, in 2041. She is not a real person; I use her as an example of an employee whose work my system could take over. For twenty years she has answered simple HR questions about leave, pay and schedules. Her employer buys my system, which takes over exactly those questions. What she needs is a chance to grow, by being retrained for another role. That also helps the client, because an employee who knows the company is often cheaper to retrain than a new one is to recruit.

**North, what now.** So my answer to my central question is yes, but only if I build the business in a way that lets people grow. In the long run, I want my working life to count for making sure that when companies bring in AI, people grow instead of losing their work. To weigh this, I use three questions from the philosopher William MacAskill, one of the founders of 80,000 Hours: how big is the problem, how neglected is it, and can it be solved (MacAskill, 2018)? The scale is big. The IMF estimates that almost 40% of jobs worldwide are exposed to AI (Georgieva, 2024). It is partly neglected, because many companies sell AI but fewer work on what happens to the employees. Whether it is solvable, I do not know; retraining helps the people it reaches. My fit is good in part. I study HRM, built an HR chatbot and lead people at Plus, but I have never run a business. MacAskill's questions look from above and cannot see Marja, the one person whose kaleidoscope I look through. When my hours are spent, I want the people whose work I automated to be better off. I will change course if, one year after an implementation, most of the affected employees lost their jobs instead of moving to other work. Next year, I will ask my next internship to let me set up an HR chatbot. I will agree beforehand what the freed-up time is for, and check with the employees after three months. Who pays? The employees who still lose their jobs. I will advise every client to offer them outplacement through an independent recruitment agency. I cannot force it, so some may go without help, and I will be open with my clients about that risk.

**What is mine to do with the time I have.** Mine to do is to bring AI into companies in a way that gives people room to grow. Within a year, I set up an HR chatbot at my next internship, agree beforehand what the freed-up time is for, and check with the employees after three months. Later, one year after each implementation for a client, I check what happened to the affected employees. If most lost their jobs instead of moving to other work, I change direction.

### 3.2 The crossing, or Alternative organisation

**My route.** Alternative organisation. I did not walk the mudflats. The redesign below has not been carried out. It is a plan for how it could work.

**The assignment.** Investigate one real organisation, find an inherited practice that may have outlived its purpose, redesign it for a changing future, and explain what the change asks from leaders and from me. My organisation is Plus Stavoren, where I work as a shift supervisor.

**Step 1. Three traces of how Plus Stavoren organises people.** These are my own observations.

1. The schedule. Every Monday we get the schedule for the week two weeks ahead. It is in the app, but in the app you cannot see everyone. That is why it also hangs outside the canteen. That is handy: if you want to swap a shift, you can see exactly who you can and cannot ask.
2. The morning coffee. It has no fixed time, like ten o'clock. It starts when the fresh products have been restocked. So when the vegetable team is done, they always help in the dairy section, so everyone can have coffee together. Two people always stay behind at the checkout, and these are the people who started later, not at seven.
3. The new checkout system. We changed from Coop to Plus. For a long time we kept working on the old Coop checkout system, and only recently did we switch to the Plus system. Older colleagues sometimes struggle with the new system, and younger colleagues always help them.

**Step 2. The organisational fossil.** At Plus Stavoren there is no fixed way to fill a shelf. Everyone does it their own way. For example, I saw a colleague put one product on his dolly, fill it, and then go and get the next one. Putting four products on the dolly at once is faster. When I ask an older colleague why he does something a certain way, the answer is often: "I have always done it like this."

| Question | My answer |
| --- | --- |
| What problem did it once solve? | In the past there was always a store manager in the evening who led the restocking team. He saw how people worked and corrected them on the spot, so a shared way of working was not needed. |
| What has changed? | There is no store manager in the evening anymore, and nothing has replaced him. How people do their work is checked less. |
| What behaviour does it create today? | Everyone fills the shelves their own way, sometimes slower than needed. New colleagues learn it from whoever they work with, because the onboarding is weak. |
| Who benefits from keeping it? | The restocking team itself. They have freedom and are not pushed to work harder. |
| Why is it difficult to change? | People say they have always done it like this. And the team is paid the same whether they work fast or slow, so nothing in the system rewards a better way of working. |

**Step 3. A future pressure: higher wages.** Labour is getting more expensive. On 1 January 2023 the Dutch minimum wage went up by 10.15% in one step ([Rijksoverheid, 2022](https://www.rijksoverheid.nl/actueel/nieuws/2022/09/23/minimumloon-in-een-keer-met-1015-omhoog)). A supermarket works with many people around the minimum wage, so every increase makes an hour of restocking more expensive. I expect that fewer people will have to do the same amount of work. What would stop working first: the evenings get longer, and young colleagues will find the work too heavy and look for another job where they have to do less for the same wage. A restocking team where everyone works at their own pace and in their own way cannot absorb that.

**Step 4. The redesign.**

|  | Current design | Proposed design |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How shelves are filled | No shared way. Everyone works in their own way, learned from whoever they worked with. | I talk with the experienced restockers to find out how they work fastest. From that, we make one shared way of working. |
| How new colleagues learn it | From whoever they happen to work with. | Every new colleague learns the shared way during onboarding. |
| Behaviour it produces | Everyone at their own pace, sometimes slower than needed. | Everyone works the same, efficient way. |
| Role of the leader | In the past, a store manager corrected people on the spot in the evening. Now nobody does. | The leader sets the standard. Before he goes home at 17:00, when the restocking team starts, he reminds them of the shared way of working. |

**BetterWork test.**

| Factor | Effect of my proposal | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Connection | Stays the same | The team works together in the same way as before. |
| Fulfilment | Goes up | With the system you finish your work faster. |
| Agency | Goes down | Everyone now has a fixed way of working. |
| Mastery | Goes up | The system makes people better at their work. |

**Unintended consequence.** Some members of the restocking team keep using their own way of working, despite the shared method.

**Safeguard.** In the evening there are assistant store managers, often younger people like me, who make sure everything gets done. They check whether the shared way is used. When someone works differently, I would first ask why. Maybe he has found an even faster way, and then that becomes part of the shared method.

**Step 5. AI as a critical thinking partner.** I wrote my own diagnosis first (steps 1 to 4, from my own answers). Then I asked an AI assistant to challenge my assumptions, point out missing evidence and give the strongest argument for keeping the current practice.

| AI challenge | Accept or reject | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One shared way may not be faster for everyone. I only saw one colleague with one product on his dolly. | Reject | You can see the difference in who empties their container first, and that is not the person who puts one product on his dolly at a time. This is an observation, not a measured time. |
| I do not know whether the evenings really take too long. | Partly accept | On the evenings I work, we often do not finish by 20:00, closing time. But that is my own experience, not measured. Before 1 November I do not work an evening shift and I have no access to the time registration, so I could not measure it. |
| Strongest argument for keeping the current practice: in step 3 I say young people leave when the work gets too heavy. Taking away their freedom could make them leave even faster. | Reject | I think young people leave faster when the work gets too heavy. A restocking system that works well does not take away all their freedom, and when someone finds a faster way, it becomes part of the shared method. |

**Step 6. Discussing my proposal.** I discussed my proposal with the store manager of Plus Stavoren.

The question I asked: "I want to work with the experienced restockers to come up with one fixed way of filling shelves, and teach it to new colleagues during onboarding. Do you think we will finish by 20:00 more often, and what would make it difficult?"

Summary of his answer: he thinks a fixed way of working can help us finish by 20:00 more often, but it will not work every time. If everyone works in roughly the same way, the work will probably go faster and fewer mistakes will be made. What makes it difficult is getting everyone on board. Some restockers have worked their own way for years and may want to keep it that way. Some days are also busier than others. With a large delivery or too few staff, we quickly fall behind. And new colleagues first need to be onboarded well, which takes time.

What this tells me: he confirms the unintended consequence I expected, that experienced restockers may stick to their own way. He also adds two things my design does not solve: busy days with a large delivery or too few staff, and the time it costs to onboard new people well.

**Evidence.** The chat with the store manager (Appendix E).

**Step 7. What this asks from leaders and from me.** From the store manager, the redesign asks that he sets the standard and reminds the team of it before he goes home at 17:00. From me, as an assistant store manager in the evening, it asks that I check whether everyone uses the shared way of working. That is not always easy, because the restocking team is younger than me, and some of them know me from QVC and do not always take me seriously. So I will first ask why someone works differently, and only then correct.

**My leadership experiment.**

| Situation | Action | Observable evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| My next evening shift with the restocking team at Plus Stavoren | I ask every restocker how they work, before I correct anyone | I keep track of who is faster, so I can see which way of working empties a container first |

**Final sentence.** This organisation will only become future-ready if its leaders are willing to ask the people on the floor how they work, and turn the best way into a shared way of working.

#### Choice assignment 1 · Why be moral?

**1. The ring.** If I had the ring for the rest of my life, I would use it to cheat on a test. Not on every test, but on a hard subject that I have already failed. For me that is social law. I have failed it twice. With the ring, I would walk into the exam room, look at someone else's answers and pass. I would tell myself that it does not matter, because later, in my work in HR, I would always check the law before I give an employee an answer. That sounds reasonable, but I know it is an excuse. It is exactly what Glaucon says: people behave well because they are watched, and when the watching stops, they find a reason why the rule does not apply to them.

**2. The argument.** Socrates gives three answers to the question why you should be just when nobody is watching. I chose the third. In my own words: most of what we call pleasure is only the end of pain, like the relief you feel when something difficult is over. Real pleasure comes from understanding and learning something yourself (Plato, ca. 375 B.C.E./1969, 583b–588a).

Does this move me? Partly. If I passed social law with the ring, I would be happy, because I would never have to study for it again. I do not like the subject, so relief would be enough for me. But I could not be proud of it, because I did nothing for it myself. That is where Socrates is right. Relief and pride are not the same. Relief is the pain stopping. Pride comes from having done it yourself, and the ring takes that away. So I do not agree that cheating always damages the person who does it, but I do see that it takes something from me that I cannot get back: being able to say that I did it myself.

**3. Myself.** A real moment when nobody could see me was at Plus Stavoren, where I work as a shift supervisor. I had had a long day and still had to work. When nobody was watching, I took a short break in the back storage room, hidden behind the containers. In the evening there is no store manager, so nobody saw it.

This fits uncomfortably with what I wrote in 3.2. There I say that I will check whether the restocking team works in the shared way. If I saw one of them hiding behind the containers, I would be more lenient, because I know I do it myself. I would just ask: "Long day?" That fits how I want to lead, asking first, but it also has a risk. If I am lenient because I do the same, I might stop correcting anyone.

Am I afraid for my own character when nobody can see me? My first answer was no, because nobody sees me anyway. But that does not fit with what I said about the ring and social law. I think it depends on the situation. After my break, I finished my work. With cheating, I would get something I did nothing for. The first does not worry me. The second does, because I would be the one person who knows it was not mine.

#### Choice assignment 2 · Which animal do you manage?

**The moment.** On a busy summer evening at Plus Stavoren, a lot of stock had come in, the restocking team was big and the shop was full, so it was quite chaotic. As a shift supervisor, I tried to keep the overview and wrote down on a note everything that had to be done. I agreed with the other assistant store manager who would do what, and then I explained the tasks to everyone. Despite the chaos, we finished on time.

**As De Waal would explain it.** Frans de Waal would say that the evening went well because people want to work together. Cooperation and helping each other are older than we are; we share them with other primates. A good leader does not force people, but keeps the peace and gives the group what it needs to cooperate (de Waal, 2017). In his view, my note and my explanation only made it easier for the team to do what they already wanted to do. And when someone was finished, they came to me to ask what else they could do. De Waal would see that as willingness, not as obedience.

**As Wrangham would explain it.** Richard Wrangham would say that the evening went well because there was structure. In his view, our peaceful behaviour is not something we simply have; groups learned to enforce it on each other (Wrangham, 2019). On that evening, the order came from the tasks we handed out. People mainly did what they were told. Without the note, the division of tasks and someone in charge, the evening would have stayed chaotic.

**My choice.** I believe Wrangham's explanation more. Most people did what they were told, and when they were finished, they did not help someone else on their own but came to me for a new task. Now and then I also had to push them to keep working. That does not look like cooperation that comes naturally. What would change my mind is an evening where everyone really comes on their own, helps each other without being asked, and keeps working without me pushing. This choice also rubs against my own North. There I say that I ask first, which assumes that people want to cooperate when you involve them. Maybe that is the honest answer: I ask first because I want people on my side, but on a busy evening I still need structure to get the work done.

#### Choice assignment 4 · De Mentale Wasstraat

**East, the situation.** I took part in De Mentale Wasstraat, an experience that helps people cope better with stress and setbacks (De Mentale Wasstraat, n.d.). I did the breathwork and the ice bath. For the ice bath, you worked in pairs with a buddy. Number one went into the ice bath first, and the buddy helped them stay calm. Then we switched. The water was ice cold.

**West, what I learned.** My buddy did two things. He reminded me to keep breathing calmly, and he supported me by calling out things like "Hold on" and "You can do it." When it was my turn to be the buddy, I did the same for him.

**South, who I was.** I was on both sides: the one who needed help, and the one who helped. In the water, I noticed how much it helped that someone stood next to me and kept pushing me in a positive way. It reminded me of the boy at the ski lift in Austria (Story 4), when I told him I was sitting right behind him. This time, I was the one who needed it.

**North, what I take from it.** Pushing people in a positive way works very well; now I have felt it myself. I will take this with me to my youth football team. When my players find something hard, I want to keep pushing them in a positive way, like my buddy did for me in the ice bath.

### Appendix C · Group reflection Film Night, The Big Short (3.1)

This is the reflection our film night group wrote together. My own part is in 3.1.

For our Film Night, we chose The Big Short (2015), focusing on the character Michael Burry, played by Christian Bale. The film shows the events leading up to the financial crisis of 2007–2008 and follows several people who realise that the American housing market is much less stable than most people believe.

We found Michael Burry particularly interesting to discuss because his leadership style is quite different from what we normally imagine when we think of a leader. He is intelligent, independent and very confident in his own analysis, but he is not particularly social or collaborative. This made it difficult for us to immediately decide whether he represents previous-generation or next-generation leadership.

**Our understanding of previous- and next-generation leadership.** Before discussing Burry, we realised that we first needed to agree on what we actually meant by previous-generation and next-generation leadership. At first, we associated previous-generation leadership mainly with hierarchy, authority and a leader making decisions independently. We saw this as a more traditional model in which the leader has the knowledge or position and others are expected to follow. We saw next-generation leadership as more focused on collaboration, empathy, reflection and involving other people in decision-making. In this model, leadership is less about having all the answers yourself and more about bringing people together and creating shared ownership. However, during our discussion we realised that the difference is not simply between "bad old leadership" and "good new leadership". Some qualities of traditional leadership can still be valuable. For example, a leader sometimes needs to make a difficult decision and stand behind it, even when other people disagree. This became especially clear when we discussed Michael Burry.

**Our discussion about Michael Burry.** Our group had different opinions about Burry's leadership. Some of us initially saw him as a typical previous-generation leader. He makes decisions largely by himself, trusts his own expertise and does not seem very interested in getting everyone involved. He can also be difficult to communicate with and does not seem to prioritise relationships with his colleagues. Others in the group viewed him differently. We argued that Burry also demonstrates qualities that we associate with next-generation leadership. He questions the established system instead of simply accepting what everyone else believes. He looks for the truth himself and is willing to challenge a very powerful industry. The most interesting part of our discussion was whether a leader can be considered a good leader if they are not collaborative. Some of us believed that collaboration is essential because leadership should involve other people. If a leader only trusts their own judgement, they can easily become isolated and miss important perspectives. Others argued that Burry's independence is exactly what makes him successful. If he had simply followed the opinions of his colleagues or the financial industry, he would never have discovered what was happening in the housing market. In the end, we agreed that both perspectives were valid. Burry shows us that leadership is not one-dimensional. Someone can have very strong leadership qualities while also having weaknesses in other areas.

**Our ranking of the five NGL qualities.** After discussing the film, we ranked the five Next Gen Leadership qualities from strongest to weakest: 1. Confident, 2. Reflective, 3. Articulate, 4. Engaging, 5. Collaborative. We placed Confident first because this is probably Burry's most obvious quality. He is willing to stand behind his analysis even when almost everyone around him thinks he is wrong. He continues to believe in his position despite pressure from his investors and colleagues. We placed Reflective second because Burry does not simply accept what other people tell him. He investigates the mortgage market himself and questions the assumptions that everyone else seems to accept. His ability to think independently is one of the main reasons he is able to predict the crisis. We ranked Articulate third. Burry clearly understands his own argument and is able to explain his reasoning, but he is not always good at communicating with people on an emotional or personal level. We placed Engaging fourth. He is not particularly charismatic and does not seem to motivate people through empathy or emotional connection. People follow his decisions mainly because of his expertise and conviction. Finally, we placed Collaborative last. Burry is extremely independent and often makes decisions based on his own analysis. He does not appear to need much input from others. While this independence is one of his strengths, it also limits his ability to create shared ownership.

**The scene we chose.** The scene we found most relevant was when Burry is analysing the mortgage data and starts to realise that the housing market is built on much riskier mortgages than people understand. We chose this because it clearly shows his Reflective quality. Instead of accepting the general belief that the housing market is safe, Burry looks deeper into the numbers. He notices patterns that other people have overlooked and starts questioning the assumptions behind the entire system. What stood out to us was that he was not looking for information that confirmed what everyone already believed. He was willing to reach a conclusion that was completely different from the general opinion. The scene also connects strongly to his confidence. Once he believes his analysis is correct, he is willing to act on it, even though doing so puts his reputation and his investors' money at risk.

**What we learned from the film.** One of our biggest takeaways was that being a strong individual does not automatically make someone a complete leader. Michael Burry is extremely confident and intellectually independent. He sees something that most other people fail to see. Without those qualities, he probably would not have recognised the coming financial crisis. At the same time, the film made us realise the limitations of his leadership style. He does not naturally bring people together, and he does not always communicate in a way that makes others feel included. This made us rethink what next-generation leadership actually means. We initially thought that being independent and challenging the status quo was enough to make someone a next-generation leader. After our discussion, we think it is more complicated. For us, next-generation leadership combines having the courage to think differently with the ability to involve and connect with other people. Burry has the first part very strongly, but he struggles with the second. Overall, we therefore see Michael Burry as a mixture of previous- and next-generation leadership. His way of working is individualistic and traditional in some ways, but his willingness to question established systems and challenge the majority makes him progressive in others. The film ultimately showed us that leadership is not just about being the person who is right. It is also about how you communicate your ideas, how you involve other people and how you respond when people disagree with you.
